Digging deeper into the Stanford port

Spacewar at Stanford, and the first tournament

One thread of Spacewar!'s long journey runs west to the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (SAIL), where the game became the centre of a working culture and then, on one evening in 1972, the occasion for what is often called the first video-game tournament. The same place produced the definitive multi-player version of the game and the article that first carried hacker culture to a mass audience.

the lab and the program
SAIL and Ralph Gorin

The game goes west

Spacewar! travelled to Stanford with the people who had made it: Steve Russell, one of the original MIT authors, brought it to the Stanford computers in the mid-1960s. At SAIL, the laboratory John McCarthy had founded in the hills above the campus, it found a permanent home on the lab's PDP-10. The version that mattered was maintained by Ralph Gorin, the lab's head system programmer and, in Stewart Brand's words, its "most avid Spacewar nut".

Gorin's Space War, revised in September 1971 and again in February 1972 with R. Taylor, was a long way from the two-ship duel of 1962. It supported up to five ships at once, with a richer physics and a set of options a player could turn on or off. Its source survives and is hosted here, recovered from the Stanford SAILDART archive and cleaned back to readable assembler.

the 1972 olympics
19 October 1972

The Intergalactic Spacewar Olympics

On the evening of 19 October 1972, the writer Stewart Brand, founder of the Whole Earth Catalog, brought a case of beer and a Rolling Stone sponsorship to SAIL and ran the Intergalactic Spacewar Olympics. Gorin ran the PDP-10 and set up the game. Three events were played on the lab's display screens: a five-player free-for-all, a two-against-two team match, and singles duels. The free-for-all went to Bruce Baumgart, a graduate student; the team title to Tovar and Robert E. Maas; the singles to Tovar. The prize was a year's subscription to Rolling Stone, and the beer.

What makes the evening matter is less the result than the report. Brand's article, "SPACEWAR: Fanatic Life and Symbolic Death Among the Computer Bums" (Rolling Stone, 7 December 1972), opens with a sentence that reads now like a prophecy: "Ready or not, computers are coming to the people. That's good news, maybe the best since psychedelics." It was among the first times the closed world of the research labs, its games and its values, was described from the inside for a general readership. The Olympics are now widely cited as the first video-game tournament.

code as instrument
The reading

A game that edits its own rules

The Stanford version went furthest of all the ports in one direction: it let the players change the game while it ran. Gorin's source carries a routine, GETNAM, that reads named parameters from the console and writes them into the running program, so the block of constants that fixes gravity, fuel, torpedo speed and the rest became a live editor for the rules of the world. Where the MIT game hard-coded its physics, the SAIL game exposed it.

This is the same move the laboratory made with everything else it touched: the artefact is not a fixed object but a configurable instrument, opened up for the user to retune. The Olympics could set "expert" parameters precisely because the game had been built to be reset. Read as code, Gorin's Space War is less a program than a small piece of apparatus, the constants block grown into a console.

A game's other inheritance

The moment the culture went public

If Cambridge shows Spacewar! as an argument that persuaded a laboratory to take interactive graphics seriously, Stanford shows it as a culture becoming visible to itself. Brand's piece named that culture, its "computer bums", its ethic of play as serious work, its conviction that the machine belonged in the hands of individuals, and put it in front of a readership that had no idea the research computers were already being used this way. The line from this evening runs directly to the personal-computer movement that Brand would go on to help define.

The game written "mainly for fun" at MIT had, ten years and three thousand miles later, become the thing a generation pointed to when it wanted to say what computers were really for.
