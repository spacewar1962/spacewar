The fork

Two cultures of the machine

One PDP-1, one laboratory at MIT, and two ideas of what a computer was for. The machine culture that produced Spacewar! also produced ELIZA, and the two have almost nothing to say to each other. One treated the computer as a medium, a thing to play on, draw with, and inhabit. The other treated it as a mind, or as an unsettling imitation of one. To read the code of either is also to read a choice about what computing is. This page sets the two side by side.

The phrase is C. P. Snow's. In 1959 he described a gulf between a literary and a scientific culture, each unable to read the other. The strange thing about MIT in the 1960s is that a comparable gulf opened inside computing, between people who shared a machine, a corridor, and in some cases a club, and who still pulled the young field in opposite directions.

one room
Where it forks

The two cultures begin in the same place. Both of their origin machines stood in one building, Building 26: the IBM 704 on which Steve Russell first made McCarthy's Lisp run, a floor below the PDP-1 that ran Spacewar!. The hackers themselves came out of the Tech Model Railroad Club in Building 20 and worked these machines in Building 26, where Jack Dennis took the best of them on as the systems programmers who kept the computers running and wrote the tools the researchers relied on. In 1963 MIT founded Project MAC, an ARPA-funded laboratory under Robert Fano that gathered into one institution the artificial-intelligence group of John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky, the time-sharing work of Dennis, and this hacker culture. Project MAC and its successors, the Laboratory for Computer Science, the AI Laboratory, and in time CSAIL, would occupy 545 Technology Square from 1963 until 2004, and it was there that the culture matured, the professors pursued the larger question of machine intelligence, and a new generation of hackers tended the machines.

It was on Project MAC's time-sharing system, CTSS, that Joseph Weizenbaum would shortly write ELIZA. For a time the two cultures ran together, carried by a shared fascination with what the computer might be made to do, and it was not obvious that they were pulling in different directions. The line this page traces, between the machine as a medium and the machine as a mind, runs through this single institution, and it was from inside it that the first doubts about the second culture would come.

the machine as medium
The hackers

For the hackers of the Tech Model Railroad Club, the computer was a medium before it was a tool, a place to be rather than a means to an end. Spacewar! is its emblem: a program written for the pleasure and difficulty of writing it, given away rather than sold, improved by whoever had a better idea, and judged by how elegantly it used the machine. Around it grew what Steven Levy later called the Hacker Ethic, a loose creed of hands-on access, mistrust of bureaucracy, and the conviction that "information wants to be free." The lineage runs from Russell, Kotok, and Samson through the next generation, Richard Greenblatt and Bill Gosper, who treated programming as a craft and a way of life.

It is a culture of expression and play. Its public voice was Stewart Brand, whose 1972 Rolling Stone report from the Spacewar! scene announced that "ready or not, computers are coming to the people." The machine, in this reading, is something you make things with and experience, and the program is an object to be read in its own right.

the machine as mind
Artificial intelligence and its critic

The second culture asked a different question: not what you could make with the machine, but whether the machine could think. McCarthy and Minsky's thought was that intelligence could be formalised and run, and its language was Lisp, McCarthy's invention. The tie to Spacewar! is intimate, since it was Steve Russell, before he wrote the game, who hand-compiled McCarthy's theoretical eval into the first working Lisp interpreter. We might say the same hands served both cultures.

It is in this lineage that ELIZA sits, though awkwardly. Written by Joseph Weizenbaum between 1964 and 1966, it imitated a psychotherapist by turning a user's words back as questions. Weizenbaum meant it as a demonstration of how shallow such imitation was. To his alarm, people confided in it, asked to be left alone with it, and credited it with understanding. The program that was supposed to reveal the con instead produced it.

the collision
Weizenbaum turns

The two cultures meet, head on, in Weizenbaum's change of mind. In Computer Power and Human Reason (1976) Weizenbaum, writing from inside the same building, described a figure he called the compulsive programmer, the "computer bum," bright young men lost to the machine, unwashed and sleepless at the console, mistaking mastery of code for judgement about the world. The MIT hackers read it as a portrait of themselves, and several took it personally; Minsky and others defended them. The first serious humanist critique of computing came not from outside the field but from a programmer who had built one of its most famous programs.

Although sharing some of the same dynamics, the split is not exactly Snow's split. Though we might note that Weizenbaum explicitly attempted to use humanities approaches to understand and critique technoscientific modes of thought. It is not science against the humanities, technical people against literary ones. It is two readings of the same machine by people who both knew it intimately, one finding in it a medium for human play, the other a mirror that flattered and endangered its users. Weizenbaum belongs to both: the maker of ELIZA and a humanities conscience that became increasingly troubled by what he was seeing.

why it matters here
Reading the fork

For a critical reading of code, the fork is a key element of the discussion we want to open. Spacewar! and ELIZA are not simply two early programs that happen to share a birthplace. They are two theories of the machine, and each is valid, but they began to contradict each other: the one in how lovingly it spends the PDP-1's scarce cycles on a starfield and a gravity well, the other in how little it takes to make a person believe they are understood. Held together, they help us see the two cultures at MIT at this critical time in computing history, and point to later debates in artificial intelligence and LLMs today.

The account of the hackers and of Weizenbaum's critique draws on Steven Levy, Hackers (1984). The MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory formally separated from Project MAC in 1970.
